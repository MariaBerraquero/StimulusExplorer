Adelson & Movshon's (1982) plaids are compound stimuli formed by superimposing two sinusoidal gratings moving in different directions. In a Type I plaid, the pattern motion direction falls within the angle formed by the two component directions. This typically occurs when both components have the same speed (or similar spatial frequency). Perception is coherent: the observer sees a single pattern moving in a direction intermediate between the two components.

About

In a Type II plaid, the components have different speeds (achieved by using distinct spatial frequencies but the same temporal frequency, or combinations that produce this asymmetry). The geometric result is that the perceived direction falls outside the angle formed by the two component directions. This is counterintuitive: the pattern moves in a direction that is not "between" the two gratings.

About

Cross-scale interaction in motion processing: the presence of a component at one spatial frequency alters the perceived direction of the other component. This is not easily explained by simple linear models of motion.

As an example, we use the experiment by Derrington & Henning (1987), a classic study on visual motion perception that investigated how the human visual system integrates motion information across different spatial scales. The central idea is as follows: observers were presented with a compound grating formed by summing two sinusoidal gratings — a static low spatial frequency component and a moving high spatial frequency component. What they found was that when the stimulus was presented for very short durations (less than 75 ms), observers systematically perceived the pattern as moving in the opposite direction.
